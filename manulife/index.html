01 · New client implementation

Three emails, from “what’s changing” to “you’re covered”

A new client means hundreds of members hearing about a new benefits provider at once. Each email has one job.

“Build and customize member marketing communications for AdminAdvantage clients, supporting: new client implementations.”
My thinking
  • Most members won’t think much about who administers their plan. They want to know if their prescription still works on June 1, so that answer goes near the top.
  • Each email has one job: the first explains what’s changing, the second asks for one action, and the third confirms they’re covered. Short emails are more likely to be read to the end.
  • Everything client-specific is a merge field, so the same three emails can be reused for the next client with small changes.
02 · Annual enrolment

An enrolment campaign that stops emailing people who are done

Open, halfway, last day. The reminders only go to members who haven’t enrolled yet.

“Annual enrolment and re-enrolment campaigns.”
My thinking
  • A reminder to someone who already enrolled can make them think it didn’t save, and teaches them to skip our emails. So emails 2 and 3 go only to members who haven’t finished.
  • Subject lines lead with the deadline. “Enrolment closes Friday” tells members what to do before they even open it.
  • Every email says what happens if they do nothing, because many members want to know that first.
03 · Members on leave

A letter for someone who has a lot going on

One template, three kinds of leave. Pick one and the middle of the letter changes. The rest stays the same.

“Develop and customize member-facing communications, including: emails and letters for members on leave.”

How the template is built

  • Fixed: greeting, what this letter is, who to call. Approved once.
  • Swaps by leave type: the one middle block. Reviewed per client, because plans differ.
  • Merge fields: name, dates, client contact.
  • Never guessed: what coverage continues. That comes from the client’s plan, so it goes in a merge field and is filled in from the plan.
My thinking
  • Someone on parental or disability leave is tired, busy or unwell. The letter has to be readable in under a minute.
  • The tone is warm and short. “Congratulations” fits parental leave but would be wrong for disability leave, so the tone changes with the middle block.
  • Coverage details change the most between clients, so they always come from the client’s plan and are checked for each client.
04 · Apology and service recovery

Say sorry like a person

A sample issue: a system error held up some dental claims for a week. Here’s the same email, twice.

“Apology and service recovery communications.”
Before

Subject: Notice regarding claims processing

We are writing to inform you that due to unforeseen technical difficulties, the adjudication of certain claims may have experienced delays. We apologize for any inconvenience this may have caused. Please be advised that claims are now being processed in the normal course. Should you have any questions, please do not hesitate to contact us.

After

Subject: Your dental claim was late. It’s paid now.

Hi {{first_name}},

Your dental claim from {{claim_date}} took longer than it should have. A system error held up some claims for about a week. That’s on us, and we’re sorry.

Your claim is now paid. You don’t need to do anything. The payment should reach you by {{payment_date}}.

If something still looks wrong, call us at {{client_phone}} and mention this email. We’ll sort it out on that call.

My thinking
  • The member wants to know two things: is my money okay, and do I have to do anything? So both answers are in the subject and the first lines.
  • One plain sentence explains what happened. “A system error held up some claims” is clearer than “unforeseen technical difficulties”.
  • “Any inconvenience this may have caused” sounds unsure. If the claim was late, the email should say so once and then explain the fix.
  • Wording like this would always go through compliance and the client before it’s sent. Plain wording still gets the full review.
05 · Client-specific FAQs

An FAQ that only answers that client’s questions

Tick what the client’s plan includes. The FAQ is put together from answers that are already approved, and anything that doesn’t apply is left out.

“Client-specific and member-friendly FAQs.”

Each answer is written once, approved once, and reused across clients.

My thinking
  • A generic FAQ often answers questions a client’s members don’t have. If a client has no flex plan, a flex question only confuses people.
  • Questions use a member’s own words, like “Can I add my partner?” instead of “Dependent eligibility”.
  • The most useful FAQ questions come from member calls. I’d check what members are calling about and add those first.
06 · Pre-send quality check

Nothing goes out with a {{blank}} in it

Paste any member email, or try the sample. It catches the small errors that are easy to miss on a second read. Then a person works through the checklist.

“Perform quality checks to ensure all communications are accurate, complete, and client-approved prior to distribution.” · “Deploy templated email communications, ensuring accuracy, consistency, and alignment with brand and compliance guidelines.”

Then a person checks

My thinking
  • The most common mistakes in templated email are small ones: a blank merge field, the wrong client name, last year’s date. A simple check can catch those, which leaves more time for a person to check the rest.
  • The check only makes a list of things to look at. A person still decides, and an email is only ready once the client has approved it.
  • Plain language is part of quality too. If a member has to look up “adjudication”, the email hasn’t done its job, even if every fact is right.
07 · Translations

200+ languages needs a system

One enrolment email going out in several languages. Each one moves through the same steps. Click a status to move it along.

“Coordinate translations in 200+ languages, working with internal and external partners to support diverse member populations.”

My thinking
  • Translation only starts once the English is locked. Any change after that has to be made in every language, which adds cost and room for mistakes.
  • Merge fields and dates are marked “do not translate” before sending, so {{first_name}} doesn’t come back translated.
  • Each language gets a second reader who speaks it, even a colleague, so mistakes are caught before members see them.
  • The due date for translations is set from the send date backwards, so the slowest language decides when English has to be final.
08 · Timelines and updates

Every communication, where it is, and what it’s waiting on

One tracker for all client comms. Anything late turns red automatically, counted from today’s date in the sample (Nov 26, 2026).

“Track timelines, manage updates, and support documentation to ensure communications are delivered on schedule.”
CommunicationClient SampleStageWaiting onStage dueSend dateStatus
My thinking
  • Most comms slip while waiting for a client to approve. So “Waiting on” is a person’s name, and each stage has its own due date as well as the send date.
  • Every change after approval gets logged: what changed, who asked, and the date. If a client asks why something changed, the answer is easy to find.
  • I use the same weekly rhythm I use with my own clients: drafts out on a fixed day, review by a fixed time, so nobody has to ask “where is it?”
09 · Making it better

Three small fixes I’d suggest after a month

Nothing big. Each one makes the next template faster to build and easier to read.

“Contribute to continuous improvement efforts by identifying opportunities to streamline communication templates and processes.”
1

A plain-language swap list

One shared list of words members don’t use, and what to say instead. Everyone writes the same way, and reviews get faster.

2

Blocks, not whole templates

Greeting, deadline, “what to do”, contact. Approved once as blocks in Stensul, then put together per client, so one fix updates every email.

3

A member question log

The top questions from calls and emails each month, added to the FAQs and the next campaign. Fewer calls on the same topic would show it’s working.

The swap list, to start

My thinking
  • I’d spend the first month learning the templates and the compliance rules before suggesting anything. Some odd wording is there for a reason.
  • These are small on purpose. Small changes are easier to get approved and quicker to put in place.
