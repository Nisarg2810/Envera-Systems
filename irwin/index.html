Your First Reader Is a Model Now: How Megan Light Writes Disclosures With AI in Mind

Draft for the Irwin blogBased on Winning IR S6E067 min read

A portfolio manager covering a few dozen companies can't read every release that lands in their inbox. Megan Light thinks a model is increasingly doing that first pass for them, and she has stopped treating it as a future problem. "I assume at this point, most of the time any material I make is going to be filtered through AI before it gets to an analyst," she says.

Light is Vice President of Investor Relations at NextDecade, a natural gas liquefaction company, a role she's held since August 2023. Before that she led IR, media relations and community relations at Archaea Energy from going public through the company's sale, and was Director of Investor Relations at Cheniere Energy. In total she's spent 20 years in energy, across upstream, midstream, LNG, oilfield services and renewable natural gas. That's a long time in an industry she once had no plans to join: "I always swore I would never move to Houston, I would never work in energy. And that is exactly where I landed."

In her conversation with Mark Fasken, she covers how energy IR has changed, why the generalist investor is a multi-year project, how her team plans its own non-deal roadshows, and what it means in practice to write for AI.

Listen to Megan Light's Winning IR episode

The game of telephone between your release and the investor

Light's starting point is volume. Over her career, the amount of information investors have to process has only gone up. She saw "a huge step change during COVID when everything switched to virtual," and that increase "never went away." AI now sits on top of it.

So she treats every deliverable as something that has to survive a relay before a person reads it:

"I just assume that I have to get through this game of telephone from my deliverable through the AI to the investment community. And is what's going through that game of telephone what I want to be going through it?"

That question, whether the summary matches what you meant, is the whole idea. A release can be accurate and still come out the other side of a model with the wrong emphasis, a missing number, or the risk factor sitting where the headline should be.

How she tests it today, after the fact

Mark asked whether the process is to write a release, run it through a model, see what comes back and adjust it before it goes out. Light's answer: that's where she wants to get to, but it isn't where she is.

"That is the ultimate goal. So right now we are only back testing because of security reasons and MNPI."

Material non-public information can't go into a tool that isn't secured for it, and NextDecade doesn't yet have an internal model she can use before publication. So her team works backwards. Once a disclosure is public, they ask the models the kind of question an analyst would, for example "Analyze this company. What are the big points in its latest disclosure?", and compare the answer with what they meant to say. Where they have close relationships with investors, they also ask them directly. Then they adjust the next quarter's materials.

The changes that come out of it aren't exotic. Light says the main lesson, "to like beat a dead horse," is "just being clear and concise and easy to understand."

A simple version of the back-test

If you want to try what Light describes without new tools, a minimal version fits into the week after earnings. This is our outline, not hers:

  1. Before the release goes out, write down the three or four points you most want an investor to take away. Keep the list with the release.
  2. Once it's public, ask two or three widely used AI assistants the same open question, something like "What are the most important points in this company's latest results?"
  3. Compare each answer with your list. Note what was dropped, what was added, and what got more weight than you intended.
  4. Keep the notes quarter over quarter. One odd summary is noise. The same miss three quarters in a row is a writing problem you can fix.

Only test public documents. That's the line Light draws too.

Simple is the strategy, even when the business isn't

Clear writing matters more because attention is scarce. "We're in an inattention economy right now, and it is increasingly difficult to get people's focus," Light says. Her answer is to have news when you want attention, make that news easy to understand, and repeat yourself on purpose: clear objectives, regular updates against them, and the same routine slides and touch points on earnings calls and at conferences.

Energy makes that harder. NextDecade's LNG business has a construction period of four to five years, its own commercial structure and its own regulatory structure, and every other energy company is different again. Light's approach is to be explicit about two things: what NextDecade has in common with its direct peers, and what actually sets it apart. Or, as she puts it, "What is that true investment thesis that makes the company worth spending that time on putting the money to work with?"

The elusive generalist is a three-year project

Energy has become a smaller part of the S&P 500, and there are far fewer energy-dedicated funds than there used to be. That pushes IR teams toward generalist investors, an idea that comes up in sell-side notes every few years. "In energy, sometimes we call this the elusive generalist investor," Light says, because it's much easier to talk about than to do.

In her experience, a generalist is "a minimum two to three-year investment, if not longer, before getting them into the stock." Her team sets meetings knowing a new name may not show up in the shareholder register for three or four years, and treats that as the cost of changing the shareholder base over time. Feedback from those meetings goes back to management: is the story simple enough, are we reaching the right people, are we on the road enough?

She's also looking at a different way to find targets: screening for companies with similar financial characteristics, even if the businesses have nothing in common, and then approaching their investors. NextDecade has 20-year take-or-pay contracts, which are rare in energy but common in infrastructure. "So we do frequently call ourselves energy infrastructure, but we should be out there talking to investors that do other types of infrastructure as well."

Why she plans her own NDRs

NextDecade still does conferences and sell-side roadshows. On top of that, Light's team now plans non-deal roadshows in-house, and the reason is control over who is in the room. When a bank or a covering analyst organises the trip, the meetings can lean toward that firm's priority clients. "[W]hen you're planning it internally, you have a hundred percent control over who you contact, who you reach out to."

The team behind it is Light and one analyst, who had run self-planned roadshows at a previous firm. They split one main target list by geography: Toronto and Chicago so far, London next, and possibly Boston later in the year. Their third self-planned NDR was scheduled for October.

She's careful not to oversell it. "It is a big resource requirement. It's not something that's gonna be right for everybody." It works for NextDecade partly because the company has eight research analysts covering it, which leaves calendar space. With 25 covering analysts, she points out, you'd already be choosing which conferences to skip.

Execution fixes valuation

Asked what separates an IR team with a seat at the table from a reactive one, Light starts with the business: "I believe that execution fixes valuation in almost every case." IR's part is to see across the company and across the market, and to bring back useful outside views, such as how a similar move by another company was received.

That also means filtering. "Not every piece of feedback that I get is worth telling my team," she says, and knowing which is which comes from time and exposure. She's candid about the part that never goes away: "I don't know what I don't know, and that's the thing that keeps me up at night more than anything else." Her defence is to keep asking good questions, and to think about messaging several moves ahead: "Is there something we're saying today that could be a pitfall nine months from now, right?"

Why this episode is worth your time

Most of the AI conversation in IR is about what AI can do for the IR team: drafting, summarising, prepping for earnings. Light's point is about what AI is already doing to your message before an investor sees it. She hasn't solved it, and she says so. Her team can only test after publication for now, and the fixes so far are plain ones about clarity and repetition. But they're checking, quarter by quarter, which is more than most teams can say about the first reader of their own disclosures.

What is a self-planned NDR?

A self-planned non-deal roadshow is a series of investor meetings that a company's own IR team organises, instead of a bank or a covering sell-side analyst. "Non-deal" means the company isn't raising money; the goal is relationships and understanding.

The main benefit is control over who you meet. Bank-organised roadshows can lean toward the bank's priority clients, while a self-planned trip can go straight to your own target list. The cost is time: the IR team handles targeting, outreach, scheduling and logistics itself. NextDecade's VP of IR, Megan Light, runs hers with one analyst and splits one target list by city. She notes it works best for companies with lighter sell-side coverage and more open calendar time.
